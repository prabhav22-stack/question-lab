**Question:** *"Examine the importance of Public Administration 20 as a subject and as an activity."*

**Introduction:**
Public Administration, defined in the IGNOU syllabus as the study of the processes, principles and institutions through which public policies are implemented, occupies a dual identity – it is both a specialised academic discipline and a practical activity of governance. As a subject it provides the analytical tools to understand state machinery, while as an activity it translates policy into service delivery, ensuring that the state fulfills its constitutional obligations to citizens.

**Importance as a Specialized Subject of Study**
The subject equips students with theoretical frameworks such as Wilson's politics‑administration dichotomy, Weber's bureaucratic rational‑legal authority and Simon's bounded rationality, all of which are highlighted in the IGNOU text. These concepts enable a systematic analysis of organisational structures, decision‑making processes and accountability mechanisms. By integrating law, economics and sociology, the discipline fosters interdisciplinary insight essential for policy formulation, evaluation and reform, thereby preparing competent civil servants, scholars and consultants who can navigate complex public problems.

**Importance of Public Administration as an Activity**
As an activity, Public Administration operationalises democratic will. It manages public resources, implements welfare programmes, and ensures regulatory compliance across sectors. The IGNOU unit stresses that effective administration sustains the rule of law, promotes equity and enhances citizen‑state interaction. Through mechanisms such as budgeting, personnel management and performance monitoring, administrators translate legislative intent into tangible outcomes, thereby reinforcing legitimacy and public trust in governmental institutions.

**Relevance in the Era of Liberalisation, Privatisation and Globalisation (LPG)**
The LPG paradigm has re‑shaped the administrative landscape. Public Administration now coordinates market‑oriented reforms, oversees privatised service delivery, and aligns domestic policies with global standards. The IGNOU material points out that administrators must balance efficiency with social justice, negotiate public‑private partnerships, and adapt to transnational regulatory regimes. This dual challenge underscores the subject’s importance for developing adaptive capacities and ethical guidelines that safeguard public interest amidst rapid economic transformation.

**Critical Evaluation & Administrative Relevance:**
While the discipline offers robust analytical tools, critics argue that its traditional bureaucratic focus may under‑represent emerging digital governance and participatory models. Moreover, the emphasis on procedural rationality can overlook informal networks and local knowledge that influence implementation. Contemporary scholarship therefore calls for a more pluralistic curriculum that integrates e‑governance, collaborative governance and sustainability, ensuring the subject remains responsive to evolving societal needs.

**Conclusion:**
Public Administration’s significance lies in its capacity to bridge theory and practice. As a scholarly field it cultivates critical understanding of state functions; as an activity it guarantees that policies serve the public good. Recognising and updating both dimensions is essential for effective governance in a dynamic, globalised world.

**Key Thinkers & Concepts:** Woodrow Wilson, Max Weber, Herbert Simon, Dwight Waldo

**Question:** *""F.W. Taylor is the father of Scientific Management Approach". Comment."*

**Introduction:**
Frederick Winslow Taylor (1856‑1915) is widely acknowledged as the pioneer of Scientific Management, a systematic effort to replace rule‑of‑thumb administration with a science of work. In the context of the industrial revolution, Taylor sought to increase productivity, reduce waste and harmonise the relationship between workers and management. His contribution lies not merely in coining the term but in formulating a set of principles that transformed managerial practice and laid the foundation for modern management theories.

**1. Development of a True Science of Work**
Taylor’s first principle emphasised the need to study work processes scientifically rather than relying on tradition. By conducting time‑and‑motion studies, he identified the ‘one best way’ to perform each task, thereby converting labour into a predictable, measurable activity. This approach challenged the prevailing artisanal methods and introduced objective standards for performance, a core tenet highlighted in Unit 4 of the IGNOU textbook.

**2. Scientific Selection and Training of Workers**
The second principle advocated for the scientific selection of workers based on aptitude and the provision of systematic training. Taylor argued that workers should be matched to jobs that suit their capabilities, and that management must assume responsibility for training. This broke with the earlier practice of ‘any man can do the work’, fostering a partnership where efficiency gains were shared between labour and management.

**3. Division of Work and Responsibility between Management and Workers**
Taylor’s third principle delineated clear responsibilities: management plans and designs work methods, while workers execute them. By separating planning from execution, Taylor eliminated the dual role that previously burdened foremen, leading to higher specialization and accountability. This division is central to the modern organisational structure and is reiterated in the IGNOU unit as a pivotal element of scientific management.

**Critical Evaluation & Administrative Relevance:**
While Taylor’s principles revolutionised industrial productivity, they attracted criticism for mechanistic treatment of labour, neglect of human motivation and the potential for worker alienation. Subsequent scholars such as Elton Mayo highlighted the importance of social relations, and the Human Relations School argued that Taylor’s focus on efficiency alone was insufficient for sustainable performance. Nevertheless, the core ideas of standardisation, measurement and systematic training retain relevance in contemporary public administration, especially in performance‑based budgeting and e‑governance initiatives.

**Conclusion:**
In sum, Taylor’s systematic analysis of work, scientific selection of personnel and clear division of responsibilities justify his epithet as the ‘father of Scientific Management’. Although later critiques have refined and expanded his framework, the foundational principles he introduced continue to underpin modern administrative practices, confirming his enduring legacy in both private and public sector management.

**Key Thinkers & Concepts:** Frederick Winslow Taylor, Elton Mayo, Henri Fayol

**Question:** *"Discuss the general principles of administration."*

**Introduction:**
Administration is the systematic process of planning, organising, directing, coordinating and controlling organisational resources to achieve defined objectives. The classical administrative school, articulated by Henry Fayol, Gulick, Urwick and later by Mooney & Reiley, distilled this process into a set of universal principles that guide managerial action irrespective of the type or size of the organisation. These principles constitute the backbone of public administration and remain relevant for contemporary governance.

**Principles of Unity, Authority and Responsibility**
The principle of Unity of Direction asserts that activities having a common objective must be coordinated under a single plan, ensuring coherence in public policy implementation. Closely linked is the Unity of Command, which stipulates that each subordinate should receive orders from only one superior, thereby avoiding confusion and conflict of interest. Gulick’s POSDCORB framework further emphasises the matching of responsibility with authority – a manager must be empowered with the requisite authority to discharge the responsibilities assigned, otherwise accountability becomes meaningless. Together, these principles create a clear line of accountability and facilitate effective decision‑making in administrative hierarchies.

**Principles of Organisation Structure**
Departmentalisation, as articulated by Fayol, involves grouping activities into distinct units (functional, geographical or product‑based) to achieve specialisation and efficiency. The Principle of Span of Control defines the optimal number of subordinates a manager can effectively supervise; a narrow span enhances supervision while a wide span promotes autonomy. Complementing these is the Principle of Hierarchy, which establishes a clear chain of command, enabling orderly flow of information and authority. The Principle of Division of Work, derived from scientific management, advocates for task specialisation to increase proficiency and productivity within each department.

**Principles of Coordination, Delegation and Staff**
Coordination is the continuous process of integrating activities across departments to avoid duplication and conflict, a necessity in complex public organisations. Delegation, the systematic transfer of authority and responsibility to lower levels, empowers sub‑managers, fosters initiative and relieves top executives for strategic tasks. The Principle of Staff underscores the need for a supportive advisory cadre that assists line managers with expertise, ensuring that decisions are informed by technical knowledge. Together, these principles promote flexibility, responsiveness and the effective utilisation of human resources in the public sector.

**Critical Evaluation & Administrative Relevance:**
While the classical principles provide a robust framework, critics argue that their rigidity may impede innovation in dynamic environments. Unity of Command, for instance, can clash with matrix structures that require dual reporting lines. Similarly, a narrow span of control may lead to bureaucratic bottlenecks, whereas an overly wide span can dilute supervision. Contemporary public administration therefore adapts these principles, blending them with network governance and collaborative models to retain their logical foundation while enhancing adaptability.

**Conclusion:**
The general principles of administration—unity of direction and command, matching authority with responsibility, departmentalisation, span of control, hierarchy, division of work, coordination, delegation and staff—constitute a timeless blueprint for organising public enterprises. By interpreting them flexibly, modern administrators can preserve organisational coherence while meeting the complex demands of 21st‑century governance.

**Key Thinkers & Concepts:** Henry Fayol, Gulick and Urwick, Mooney and Reiley

**Question:** *"Discuss the contribution of the Human Relations 20 Approach to the theory of Administration."*

**Introduction:**
The Human Relations Approach emerged in the 1930s as a reaction to the mechanistic focus of classical administration, which emphasized structure, hierarchy and efficiency while largely ignoring the social and psychological needs of workers. Grounded in the Hawthorne experiments conducted at the Western Electric plant, the approach posits that employee attitudes, informal groups, and interpersonal relations are decisive determinants of productivity and organisational effectiveness. By foregrounding motivation, communication and leadership style, it broadened the theoretical base of administration beyond the purely technical dimensions of classical theory.

**Emergence and Core Concepts**
The movement was catalysed by Elton Mayo and his colleagues, who observed that changes in physical conditions alone did not yield sustained productivity gains. Their findings highlighted the importance of social factors such as peer influence, sense of belonging, and recognition. Key concepts introduced include the informal organisation, employee morale, and the ‘human factor’ as a strategic resource. These ideas shifted administrative thought from a deterministic view of work to a more holistic perspective that integrates human needs with organisational objectives.

**Hawthorne Studies and Principal Conclusions**
The series of experiments—namely the Illumination, Relay Assembly, and the famous ‘Bank Wiring’ studies—demonstrated that workers performed better when they perceived that management cared about their welfare. The principal conclusions, often summarised as the ‘Hawthorne Effect’, underscored that attention, feedback, and participative decision‑making enhance motivation. These empirical insights provided a scientific basis for managerial practices such as employee involvement, job enrichment, and supportive supervision, thereby embedding human‑centred policies into administrative theory.

**Contribution to Administrative Theory**
By integrating psychological insights with organisational analysis, the Human Relations Approach enriched classical administration in three ways. First, it introduced the concept of leadership as a relational process rather than merely a positional authority, influencing later theories of transformational and participative leadership. Second, it legitimised the study of informal groups and communication networks, paving the way for modern organisational culture and climate research. Third, it advocated for welfare programmes, training, and employee participation, which are now core components of contemporary public administration and human resource management.

**Critical Evaluation & Administrative Relevance:**
While the Human Relations Approach corrected the myopia of classical theory, it has been critiqued for over‑emphasising harmony at the expense of power dynamics and structural constraints. Subsequent scholars argue that it underestimates conflict, inequality, and the role of external environmental pressures. Nevertheless, its legacy endures in contemporary administrative models that balance efficiency with employee well‑being, making it a pivotal bridge between early scientific management and modern human‑resource‑oriented governance.

**Conclusion:**
In sum, the Human Relations Approach expanded administrative theory by recognising workers as social beings whose attitudes and interactions critically shape organisational performance. Its empirical foundations and managerial prescriptions continue to inform public administration practice, underscoring the enduring relevance of a people‑centric perspective.

**Key Thinkers & Concepts:** Elton Mayo, Fritz Roethlisberger, William J. Dickson, Mary Parker Follett

**Question:** *""Herbert Simon identified a prominent role for 20 choice, values and facts, in decision making". Discuss."*

**Introduction:**
Herbert A. Simon, a pioneer of the behavioural approach in public administration, reconceptualised decision‑making as a process of choice that is bounded by the decision‑maker’s values and the factual information available. In contrast to the classical rational model, Simon argued that administrators operate as "administrative man" who seeks satisfactory solutions rather than optimal ones. This answer outlines Simon’s triadic framework—choice, values and facts—explains how they interact within the hierarchy of decisions, and evaluates the contemporary relevance of his insights.

**1. Choice and the Bounded Rationality Paradigm**
Simon defined choice as the selection of an alternative from a limited set of feasible options. Because cognitive capacities and time are constrained, decision‑makers employ "bounded rationality"—they search only until a satisfactory alternative (satisficing) is found. The hierarchy of decisions (strategic, tactical, operational) illustrates how choices at higher levels set the parameters for lower‑level choices, ensuring organisational coherence. This view departs from the classical assumption of unlimited rationality and underscores the pragmatic nature of public administration.

**2. Values as the Guiding Compass**
Values represent the normative criteria that shape the desirability of alternatives. Simon emphasized that values are not external to the decision process; they filter the set of considered options and determine the satisficing threshold. In bureaucratic organisations, institutional values—efficiency, equity, legality—act as constraints that align individual choices with organisational goals. By integrating values, Simon’s model bridges the gap between technical rationality and the political‑ethical dimensions of public policy.

**3. Facts and Information Processing**
Facts constitute the empirical data on which choices are evaluated. Simon highlighted that the quality and accessibility of facts influence the size of the choice set and the accuracy of the satisficing solution. He introduced the concept of "information processing" whereby administrators convert raw data into usable knowledge through routines and, increasingly, computer‑based systems. The interplay of facts with values determines the perceived adequacy of an alternative, reinforcing the iterative nature of decision‑making.

**4. Integration within the Decision‑Making Cycle**
Simon’s model presents a cyclical process: identification of a problem, gathering of facts, evaluation against values, selection of a choice, and implementation. Execution of decisions is further shaped by organisational influence models, such as coalition building and power dynamics, which modify both the factual base and value priorities. This holistic view captures the complexity of administrative behaviour and provides a realistic template for public managers.

**Critical Evaluation & Administrative Relevance:**
While Simon’s emphasis on bounded rationality and satisficing offers a realistic portrayal of administrative behaviour, critics argue that it underestimates the role of strategic imagination and learning in complex policy environments. Moreover, the reliance on clear values may be problematic in pluralistic societies where value conflicts are pervasive. Advances in information technology have expanded the factual base, yet they also introduce information overload, challenging the assumption that more facts automatically improve decisions. Nonetheless, Simon’s framework remains foundational for contemporary governance, especially in designing decision‑support systems and in training public officials to recognise cognitive limits.

**Conclusion:**
Simon’s articulation of choice, values and facts reshapes decision‑making from an idealised rational exercise to a bounded, value‑laden, fact‑driven process. By acknowledging cognitive limits, normative criteria, and informational constraints, his theory offers a pragmatic lens for analysing and improving administrative decisions in modern public organisations.

**Question:** *"Examine Frederick Herzberg's motivation-hygiene theory and compare it with Maslow's model."*

**Introduction:**
Motivation remains a central concern of public administration because it directly influences employee performance, satisfaction and organisational effectiveness. Two seminal contributions – Abraham Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs (1943) and Frederick Herzberg’s Motivation‑Hygiene (or Two‑Factor) Theory (1959) – offer distinct yet complementary lenses for understanding why individuals work. While Maslow presents a sequential, need‑based pyramid, Herzberg differentiates between factors that cause dissatisfaction (hygiene) and those that engender true motivation (motivators). This answer outlines each theory, juxtaposes their core propositions and evaluates their relevance for contemporary public organisations.

**Herzberg’s Motivation‑Hygiene Theory**
Herzberg, based on a systematic study of engineers and accountants, identified two independent sets of determinants. Hygiene factors – salary, company policies, supervisory relations, working conditions and job security – do not motivate when present but cause dissatisfaction when absent. Motivators – achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement and the work itself – generate genuine satisfaction and higher performance. The theory posits that improving hygiene eliminates dissatisfaction, whereas enriching the job with motivators raises intrinsic motivation. Herzberg further advocated ‘job enrichment’ and ‘vertical job loading’ as practical tools for public managers to redesign posts so that employees experience greater autonomy and meaningful achievement.

**Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs**
Maslow’s model arranges human needs in a five‑level pyramid: physiological, safety, social (belongingness), esteem and self‑actualisation. According to the hierarchy, lower‑order needs must be at least partially satisfied before higher‑order needs become salient. In the public sector, basic remuneration and job security address physiological and safety needs; collegial work environments satisfy social needs; recognition and status meet esteem needs; and opportunities for personal growth, innovation and public service fulfil self‑actualisation. Maslow’s theory underscores that motivation is dynamic – as one need is satisfied, the next level emerges, prompting managers to adopt a progressive, holistic approach to employee development.

**Comparative Analysis of Herzberg and Maslow**
Both theories recognise that extrinsic and intrinsic elements shape employee behaviour, yet they differ in structure and application. Maslow’s hierarchy is a linear, developmental sequence, whereas Herzberg’s two‑factor model treats hygiene and motivators as orthogonal – the presence of one does not guarantee the presence of the other. In practice, Herzberg’s framework is more actionable for job design: eliminating hygiene deficits (e.g., inadequate pay) prevents dissatisfaction, while adding motivators (e.g., responsibility) drives performance. Maslow, by contrast, offers a broader diagnostic tool for assessing which level of need is dominant among staff, guiding policy decisions such as welfare schemes (physiological/safety) or leadership development programmes (esteem/self‑actualisation). Together, they provide a comprehensive motivational blueprint for public administrators.

**Critical Evaluation & Administrative Relevance:**
Empirical studies have shown mixed support for both models. Herzberg’s reliance on self‑reported critical incidents may suffer from recall bias, and the dichotomy between hygiene and motivators can blur in complex public‑service settings where, for example, salary also conveys status. Maslow’s strict hierarchy has been contested; individuals often pursue higher‑order needs despite unmet lower needs, especially when ideological commitment to public service is strong. Nonetheless, the theories remain valuable: Herzberg’s job‑enrichment principles underpin modern performance‑management systems, while Maslow’s need‑based perspective informs welfare and capacity‑building policies.

**Conclusion:**
In sum, Herzberg’s Motivation‑Hygiene Theory offers a pragmatic, two‑factor approach to designing motivating jobs, whereas Maslow’s Hierarchy provides a broader, need‑progression framework. Effective public administration integrates both – first securing hygiene conditions to remove dissatisfaction, then aligning job characteristics and development opportunities with the evolving needs of employees, thereby fostering sustained motivation and organisational excellence.

Victor Vroom’s Expectancy Theory, presented in the IGNOU unit on socio‑psychological approaches, explains motivation as a function of three cognitions: expectancy (effort → performance), instrumentality (performance → outcome) and valence (value of the outcome). The theory assumes that individuals make rational choices based on perceived probabilities and the attractiveness of rewards. By linking personal goals with organisational rewards, Vroom provides a systematic framework for managers to influence employee effort.

**Assumption 1 – Expectancy (Effort‑Performance Relationship)**
Vroom assumes that an employee’s belief that increased effort will lead to better performance is contingent on self‑efficacy, clarity of task requirements and availability of necessary resources. The official IGNOU text stresses that when employees perceive the task as achievable, expectancy is high, prompting greater effort. Conversely, ambiguous goals or lack of skill lower expectancy, reducing motivation.

**Assumption 2 – Instrumentality (Performance‑Reward Link)**
The second assumption posits that individuals judge the likelihood that successful performance will be rewarded. This judgment depends on trust in the organisational system, transparent reward policies and the perceived fairness of the reward structure. The IGNOU material highlights that when instrumentality is strong, employees see a direct cause‑effect between performance and outcomes, reinforcing effort.

**Assumption 3 – Valence (Reward Value)**
Valence reflects the personal importance attached to the expected reward, which may be monetary, recognition, career advancement or intrinsic satisfaction. Vroom’s theory assumes that rewards must align with individual needs; a high‑pay bonus may have low valence for an employee who values learning opportunities. The IGNOU syllabus notes that managers must identify diverse valences to design effective incentive systems.

**Implications for Managerial Practice**
From the three assumptions, Vroom derives several managerial implications: (i) clarify task expectations and provide training to raise expectancy; (ii) establish transparent performance‑reward linkages to enhance instrumentality; (iii) customise reward packages to match employee valences, thereby increasing overall motivation. The IGNOU unit further suggests that performance appraisal systems, feedback mechanisms and participative decision‑making are practical tools to operationalise these implications.

**Critical Evaluation & Administrative Relevance:**
While Vroom’s model offers a logical structure, critics argue that it over‑emphasises rational calculation and underestimates emotional, cultural and social influences on motivation. Empirical studies cited in later IGNOU readings reveal measurement difficulties in quantifying expectancy, instrumentality and valence, especially in collectivist settings where group rewards dominate. Moreover, the theory assumes stable preferences, whereas employee valences may shift with changing personal circumstances, limiting its predictive accuracy over time.

**Conclusion:**
Victor Vroom’s Expectancy Theory rests on three core assumptions—expectancy, instrumentality and valence—that together explain how individuals decide to exert effort. By translating these cognitions into managerial actions—clear goals, transparent reward systems and personalised incentives—organizations can enhance motivation. Nevertheless, the theory’s rationalist bias and contextual limitations require managers to complement it with broader socio‑psychological insights for sustainable performance.

**Question:** *"Explain Systems Approach and bring out the 20 views of David Easton on Systems Approach."*

**Introduction:**
The systems approach views an organisation or a political entity as an inter‑related set of components that function together to achieve a common purpose. Originating in the physical sciences, the approach was transferred to social sciences, notably political science and public administration, to explain how inputs are transformed into outputs through systematic interactions. In the IGNOU syllabus, the approach is presented as a departure from classical, linear models, emphasizing openness, feedback and environmental exchange.

**Meaning of Systems Approach**
A system is defined as an interconnected set of elements that operate as a whole. According to the IGNOU unit, three properties characterise a system: (i) a set of internal interactions, (ii) a boundary that distinguishes the system from its environment, and (iii) a flow of inputs and outputs that maintain equilibrium. The approach therefore stresses that organisations cannot be studied in isolation; they must be examined in relation to their external milieu, feedback mechanisms and internal subsystems.

**Organisation as an Open System**
Modern organisations are described as open systems because they continuously exchange resources, information and energy with their environment. This openness explains the growing complexity, diversification and trans‑national operations highlighted in the unit. Open‑system theory, drawing on Talcott Parsons and later scholars, posits that organisations adapt through feedback loops, thereby achieving stability while remaining responsive to external changes.

**David Easton's Twenty Views on Systems Approach**
Easton applied the systems paradigm to political systems. His twenty views, as outlined in the IGNOU material, are: 1) Politics is a system of authoritative decision‑making; 2) The system receives inputs (demands, supports) from the environment; 3) Decisions constitute the system's outputs; 4) Feedback links outputs back to inputs; 5) The system is open, interacting with its social environment; 6) Stability is achieved through equilibrium between inputs and outputs; 7) Change occurs when disequilibrium exceeds a threshold; 8) Authority is the mechanism that converts inputs into decisions; 9) The political system is differentiated into sub‑systems (legislature, executive, judiciary); 10) Each sub‑system has its own inputs and outputs; 11) The system is goal‑oriented, aiming at order and legitimacy; 12) Political culture shapes the nature of inputs; 13) Institutional arrangements determine decision pathways; 14) The system can be analysed at macro‑ and micro‑levels; 15) Power is the capacity to influence inputs; 16) Policy formulation is a conversion of demands into programs; 17) Implementation links the political system with administrative structures; 18) Evaluation of policies provides new feedback; 19) The system is dynamic, constantly adapting to societal change; 20) Comparative analysis of political systems is possible by applying the same systemic criteria.

**Critical Evaluation & Administrative Relevance:**
Easton's systemic model offers a powerful lens for analysing political stability and change, yet it has limitations. Its emphasis on equilibrium may underplay conflict and power struggles that do not fit neatly into feedback loops. Moreover, the model assumes rational processing of inputs, overlooking informal networks and elite bargaining. Contemporary scholars augment Easton’s framework with network theory and institutionalism to capture the complexity of modern governance.

**Conclusion:**
The systems approach, by treating organisations and political entities as open, interactive wholes, provides a coherent structure for analysing inputs, processes and outputs. Easton's twenty views extend this logic to political systems, highlighting authority, feedback and equilibrium as central mechanisms. While the approach remains foundational in public administration, integrating it with newer theories enhances its explanatory power for today’s multifaceted administrative realities.

**Key Thinkers & Concepts:** David Easton, Chester Barnard, Talcott Parsons

**Question:** *"What do you understand by organisational culture ? Discuss its components."*

**Introduction:**
Organisational culture refers to the shared values, beliefs, assumptions and behavioural patterns that guide how members of an organisation think and act. As highlighted in the IGNOU Unit 17 material, culture is the "stuff" that binds individuals together, providing a collective identity and a framework for interpreting the external environment. In a rapidly changing, competitive world, a strong culture enables an organisation to respond swiftly, sustain learning and create new knowledge, thereby becoming a ‘learning organisation’ (Garrett, 1987; Senge, 1990).

**1. Core Values and Beliefs**
Core values constitute the deepest layer of culture; they are the enduring principles that define what the organisation stands for. Beliefs are the shared convictions about how work should be performed and what outcomes are desirable. According to the unit, these values and beliefs shape decision‑making, influence strategic direction and provide the moral compass for employees. For example, a commitment to customer orientation or innovation becomes a cultural norm that permeates daily actions.

**2. Norms, Practices and Rituals**
Norms are the unwritten rules that dictate acceptable behaviour, while practices and rituals are the observable manifestations of those norms. The IGNOU text stresses that organisational culture is expressed through routine activities such as meetings, performance appraisal systems, and celebration of achievements. These rituals reinforce the underlying values, create a sense of belonging and facilitate the transmission of culture to new members.

**3. Symbols, Language and Stories**
Symbols (logos, dress codes, office layout) and a distinctive organisational language (jargon, slogans) serve as visible cues of culture. Stories and myths about founders, past successes or crises convey cultural meanings across generations. The unit notes that such symbolic artefacts help employees make sense of complex situations and align their behaviour with the organisational ethos.

**4. Learning Orientation and Core Competencies**
A contemporary component emphasized by Prahalad and Hamel (1990) is the focus on core competencies that constitute the collective learning of the organisation. This learning orientation is embedded in culture through continuous training, knowledge‑sharing platforms and a willingness to experiment. It ensures that culture remains dynamic, supporting the creation of new knowledge as advocated by Senge (1990).

**Critical Evaluation & Administrative Relevance:**
While the components outlined above provide a comprehensive framework, organisational culture is not static. Rapid technological change and global competition can render existing values obsolete, leading to cultural inertia. Moreover, an overly strong culture may suppress dissent and stifle innovation, contradicting the learning‑organisation ideal. Hence, managers must periodically diagnose cultural fit, encourage sub‑cultures that foster creativity, and balance stability with adaptability.

**Conclusion:**
In sum, organisational culture is the collective fabric of values, norms, symbols and learning orientations that shapes behaviour and performance. Its components—core values, norms and rituals, symbols and stories, and a learning‑oriented competency base—interact to create a coherent identity while enabling the organisation to navigate change. A nuanced understanding and deliberate management of these components are essential for sustaining relevance in today’s volatile environment.

**Key Thinkers & Concepts:** Garrett (1987), Senge (1990), Prahalad and Hamel (1990)

**Question:** *"Goals and Anti-Goals of New Public Administration."*

**Introduction:**
New Public Administration (NPA) emerged in the 1960s as a reformist response to the technocratic and efficiency‑driven paradigm of traditional public administration. It foregrounds social equity, citizen participation and value‑oriented outcomes rather than mere procedural regularity.

**Goal: Social Equity and Justice**
NPA posits that public agencies must actively reduce socioeconomic disparities by designing policies that benefit marginalized groups. This goal reflects the Minnowbrook consensus that public administration is a value‑laden enterprise, seeking distributive justice rather than neutral service delivery.

**Goal: Responsiveness and Citizen Participation**
The movement stresses that administrators should be responsive to citizens’ needs and involve them in decision‑making through participatory mechanisms such as public hearings, advisory councils and collaborative budgeting. Such engagement enhances legitimacy and aligns public services with community priorities.

**Anti‑Goal: Bureaucratic Neutrality and Technocratic Isolation**
NPA explicitly rejects the anti‑goal of treating administration as a value‑free, technocratic function detached from political and social contexts. It argues that neutrality masks power relations and hampers the pursuit of equity, thereby contradicting the core ethos of the New Public Administration.

**Critical Evaluation & Administrative Relevance:**
While the goals of equity and participation broaden democratic accountability, implementation often collides with resource constraints and entrenched bureaucratic cultures. Moreover, the anti‑goal of neutrality may be idealistic, as complete value‑free administration is practically unattainable.

**Conclusion:**
In sum, New Public Administration reorients public management toward social justice, citizen engagement and value‑driven action, while repudiating bureaucratic neutrality. Its goals remain pertinent, yet translating them into practice demands sustained political will and institutional reform.

**Question:** *"Discuss the concept, characteristics and types of 20 organisation."*

**Introduction:**
Organisation is the purposeful arrangement of people, resources and activities to achieve predetermined goals. As Katz and Kahn observe, when goal‑directed activities are coordinated rationally through assignment of duties and responsibilities, this rational coordination constitutes organisation. In public administration, organisation provides the structural backbone that aligns individual efforts with collective objectives, thereby preventing duplication, confusion and conflict. The concept therefore bridges the gap between abstract policy intent and concrete service delivery.

**Characteristics of an Organisation**
An organisation exhibits several distinctive traits. First, it is goal‑oriented; every activity is directed towards a common purpose. Second, it possesses a defined structure of authority and hierarchy, which delineates roles, responsibilities and reporting relationships. Third, it operates through a system of coordinated activities, ensuring that tasks are interdependent yet complementary. Fourth, it is a social entity, comprising individuals who interact, share norms and develop a collective identity. Fifth, it is enduring, persisting beyond the tenure of any single member, and finally, it is adaptable, capable of modifying its processes and structures in response to environmental changes.

**Formal and Informal Organisations**
Formal organisations are deliberately created by statutes, policies or managerial decisions and are characterised by written rules, official hierarchies and clearly defined procedures. Public agencies, ministries and statutory bodies exemplify formal structures. In contrast, informal organisations emerge spontaneously from interpersonal relationships, shared values and social networks within the formal framework. They influence communication patterns, morale and decision‑making, often filling gaps left by formal mechanisms. Recognising both dimensions is essential for administrators, as informal dynamics can either reinforce or undermine formal objectives.

**Typologies of Organisations**
IGNOU classifies organisations on several bases. By goal or function, they are categorized as regulatory (e.g., Election Commission), service‑oriented (e.g., Municipal Corporations) or developmental (e.g., Planning Commission). On the basis of the primary beneficiary, they are public‑oriented, private‑oriented or mixed. Compliance‑based typology distinguishes statutory bodies that must adhere to legal mandates from discretionary agencies with greater flexibility. Finally, authority‑based typology differentiates hierarchical (bureaucratic) organisations from flat or networked structures. These typologies help administrators tailor management approaches to the specific nature of each entity.

**Critical Evaluation & Administrative Relevance:**
While the classical view of organisation emphasises hierarchy and formal rules, contemporary public administration acknowledges the growing relevance of network governance, collaborative partnerships and digital platforms. The rigid typologies may obscure hybrid forms such as public‑private partnerships, where authority and beneficiary lines blur. Moreover, the focus on structural characteristics can underplay cultural and behavioural aspects that shape performance. Hence, scholars advocate a more fluid, systems‑oriented perspective that integrates formal design with informal dynamics and external stakeholder influences.

**Conclusion:**
In sum, organisation is a goal‑directed, structured, enduring and adaptable social system. Its key characteristics—goal orientation, hierarchical authority, coordinated activity, social nature, durability and adaptability—provide the scaffolding for public administration. Understanding formal versus informal dimensions and the multiple typologies equips administrators to design, manage and reform organisations that are responsive to contemporary governance challenges.

**Key Thinkers & Concepts:** Katz, Kahn, Max Weber, Henri Fayol

**Question:** *"Critically evaluate the new Public Management Approach."*

**Introduction:**
The New Public Management (NPM) emerged in the late 1970s as a response to rising government expenditure, neo‑liberal ideology, the New Right philosophy and the public‑choice school of thought. Drawing on the Washington Consensus, NPM seeks to infuse market‑oriented mechanisms, managerial efficiency and customer‑centric service delivery into the public sector. It marks a paradigm shift from the traditional bureaucratic model of rule‑bound administration to a results‑oriented, performance‑driven approach.

**Genesis and Theoretical Foundations**
NPM’s genesis is rooted in four inter‑linked forces: (i) the surge in public spending that strained fiscal balances, (ii) the diffusion of neo‑liberalism which championed deregulation and competition, (iii) the New Right’s emphasis on individual responsibility, and (iv) the public‑choice perspective that treated bureaucrats as self‑interested agents. These influences converged to promote decentralisation, contracting out, and the adoption of private‑sector management techniques such as performance contracts, benchmarking and results‑based budgeting.

**Salient Features of NPM**
Key features include: (a) managerial autonomy and a shift from rule‑bound processes to flexible decision‑making; (b) a customer‑oriented mindset where citizens are treated as ‘clients’ demanding quality and choice; (c) extensive use of performance indicators, audits and public‑private partnerships; (d) decentralisation of authority to agencies and quasi‑governmental bodies; and (e) a strong reliance on information technology for service delivery and transparency. These elements aim to enhance efficiency, effectiveness and accountability in public service.

**Impact and Appraisal of NPM Reforms**
NPM has produced mixed outcomes. On the positive side, many agencies have achieved cost‑savings, improved service standards and greater responsiveness. However, the approach has also generated a clash of values with traditional public administration, privileging managerial expertise over policy capacity. The blurring of citizen‑politician relationships, ambiguous accountability mechanisms, and the promotion of individualistic over collective interests have been widely criticised. Moreover, the customer‑orientation sometimes undermines equity, as market logic may marginalise vulnerable groups.

**Critical Evaluation & Administrative Relevance:**
While NPM succeeded in injecting efficiency and a performance culture, its over‑reliance on private‑sector analogues neglects the distinctive political and social mandates of the state. The lack of a clear accountability framework often leads to fragmented governance and reduced democratic control. Contemporary challenges—such as digital governance, sustainability and inclusive service delivery—require a hybrid model that blends NPM’s managerial tools with the normative values of traditional public administration, such as equity, participation and public interest. Hence, NPM should be viewed as a partial reform rather than a comprehensive solution.

**Conclusion:**
In sum, the New Public Management approach re‑defined public administration by foregrounding efficiency, decentralisation and customer focus. Its genesis in neo‑liberal and public‑choice thought explains its market‑centric orientation. Although it has delivered measurable gains, the approach is limited by its insufficient attention to accountability, equity and the political nature of public service. A balanced reform agenda that integrates NPM’s managerial strengths with the democratic ethos of traditional administration is essential for contemporary governance.

**Key Thinkers & Concepts:** David Osborne, Christopher Hood, Milton Friedman, James Buchanan, New Right scholars
